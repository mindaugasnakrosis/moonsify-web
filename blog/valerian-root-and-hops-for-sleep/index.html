Valerian Root & Hops for Sleep: What the Research Says

By the Moonsify Team · Published August 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Long before melatonin appeared on a supplement label, people were steeping valerian root into evening tea and stuffing pillows with hops to help them drift off. Both herbs have been used for sleep for centuries — valerian since at least ancient Greece, hops from European folk medicine. But tradition isn't evidence. What does modern research actually say?

Key takeaways

  • A systematic review of 16 randomized trials suggests valerian may improve subjective sleep quality, with a favorable safety profile.
  • Hops contains compounds studied for calming, sedative-like properties, and is traditionally paired with valerian.
  • Trials of the valerian–hops combination suggest the pair may work better together than valerian alone.
  • Herbal sleep support works best as a consistent nightly routine, not a one-off rescue.

Valerian root: the best-studied herbal sleep aid

Valeriana officinalis is a flowering plant whose root contains valerenic acid and related compounds. The leading hypothesis for how it works involves the GABA system — the brain's main "calm down" signaling pathway, the same one targeted (much more forcefully) by prescription sleep medication. Valerian compounds appear to interact with GABA receptors gently, which fits its reputation: relaxing rather than sedating.

The most cited evidence is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 randomized, placebo-controlled trials covering over 1,000 patients, published in The American Journal of Medicine. Its conclusion: valerian might improve sleep quality without producing significant side effects.1 Individual studies vary in quality and dose, and effects tend to build over consistent use rather than appearing the first night — a pattern worth knowing before you judge any valerian product on night one.

Hops: not just for beer

Humulus lupulus — the same cone-shaped flower that gives beer its bitterness — has a long parallel history as a calming herb. European herbal traditions used hops-filled pillows for restlessness, and modern pharmacology has identified compounds in hops (notably 2-methyl-3-buten-2-ol and xanthohumol derivatives) with sedative-like activity in preclinical research.2

On its own, hops has less human trial data than valerian. Where it gets interesting is in combination.

Why valerian and hops are paired

The two herbs appear repeatedly together in commercial formulas, and that's not marketing accident — it reflects the clinical research. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial found that a valerian–hops combination shortened sleep latency versus placebo, while valerian alone showed weaker effects, suggesting the hops component contributes meaningfully.3 Reviews of the combination have reached similar cautious conclusions: modest but real signals for improving sleep, with good tolerability.4

Mechanistically, researchers describe the pair as complementary: valerian working through GABA-related pathways, hops possibly also engaging adenosine and melatonin-adjacent signaling — different doors into the same quiet room.

Safety and practical notes

  • Tolerability: both herbs are generally well tolerated in studies; unlike prescription sedatives, valerian hasn't shown dependence or next-day impairment at typical doses.
  • Timing: effects are gentle and cumulative. Give any valerian-hops product a couple of weeks of consistent use before judging it.
  • Interactions: don't combine with alcohol, sedative medication or anxiety medication without medical advice — calming effects can add up.
  • Who should check first: pregnant or nursing women, anyone on regular medication, and people with liver conditions should consult a doctor before valerian use.
